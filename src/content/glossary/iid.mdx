**What it is.** Samples $(x^{(1)}, y^{(1)}), \dots, (x^{(n)}, y^{(n)})$ are i.i.d. when each is drawn from the same distribution $P$ and no draw influences another. Independence lets the probability of the whole dataset factor into a product:

$$P(\mathcal{D}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}).$$

**How ML uses it.** Every likelihood in this course starts here; taking logs turns the product into the sum that MLE maximizes. It also justifies estimating future error on a held-out set. Clinical data often break it: repeated visits from one patient are correlated, so cross-validation must split by patient, not by row. Do not confuse i.i.d. samples (independence across rows) with conditionally independent features (across columns, the Naive Bayes assumption).
